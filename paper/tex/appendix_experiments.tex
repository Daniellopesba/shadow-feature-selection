E3 reads the importances after the first $k$ boosting rounds of the E2 ensembles (\cref{fig:rounds}; a round with no split anywhere counts as dropping every feature). With the prevalence intercept most of the failure is present from the first tree: averaged over cells, the false negative rate is 0.48 after one tree, 0.36 after 10 and 0.40 after 300, so later rounds add a small amount. Under the XGBoost~2.x default at $\phat = 0.001$ the first trees do not split at all, the rate is 1.00 after three rounds and 0.72 after 300, against 0.54 and 0.57 with the prevalence intercept: the toll of \cref{prop:toll} lasts for tens of rounds, as the heuristic after it says.

\subsection{Other learners}\label{app:backends}

E5 repeats the E2 design at $\delta = 0.5$ with scikit-learn gradient boosting, random forests (full depth, depth 5, and depth 5 with class-balanced bootstrap samples) and LightGBM, each with its native importance (\cref{fig:backends}). At $\phat = 0.1$ every learner fails at small $\nmin$ much as XGBoost does (false negative rates 0.40--0.55 at $\nmin = 25$), as \cref{prop:level,cor:shadows} lead one to expect. Two differences stand out. LightGBM, which bounds children by sample count rather than by Hessian, keeps a residual effect of the prevalence at fixed $\nmin$: at $\nmin = 100$ its rate is 0.45--0.47 at $\phat = 0.01$ against 0.13--0.15 at $\phat = 0.1$; we have no account of this. And the depth-5 random forest with class-balanced bootstrap samples has the lowest rate of all learners at every $\nmin$ (0.07 and 0.16 at $\nmin = 50$, against 0.18--0.48 for the others). Its level is still $1/(K+1)$ by \cref{prop:level}, so this is a gain in power; class weights in XGBoost brought no such gain in E4.
